1. What phishing is, and why it works

Phishing is a fraud technique where an attacker pretends to be someone you trust in order to steal credentials, money or data, or to get you to install malware. It works because it targets people, not software.

Email

Phishing

Mass emails pretending to be banks, delivery firms or software vendors.

Targeted

Spear phishing

Personalized to you: your name, manager, project or supplier, often researched on LinkedIn.

Executives

Whaling and BEC

Impersonating or targeting senior leaders, often to request urgent payments (business email compromise).

SMS

Smishing

Text messages about parcels, tolls or bank alerts with a short link.

Phone

Vishing

Phone calls, sometimes with voice cloning, pretending to be IT support or your bank.

QR code

Quishing

QR codes in emails or on posters that lead to fake login pages and bypass email link scanners.

The usual goal: get you to do one of three things: enter a password, open a file, or send money or information. If a message pushes you toward any of these, slow down and verify.

2. How to recognize a phishing email

You already found the red flags in the sample above. Here they are as a working checklist. One flag is a reason for caution. Two or more is a reason to report.

Check the sender

Look at the actual address, not just the display name. Watch for lookalike domains (micros0ft, paypa1), free webmail addresses claiming to be a company, or a domain that does not match the brand.

Check the tone

Urgency, threats, prizes, secrecy, or "act now" pressure are designed to stop you from thinking.

Check the links

Hover (or long-press on mobile) to reveal the real destination before you click. The visible text can say anything.

Check the ask

Legitimate organizations rarely ask for passwords, one-time codes or payment by gift card over email.

Check the attachment

Be wary of unexpected .zip, .html, .iso, .exe, and Office files asking you to "enable macros" or "enable content".

Check the details

Generic greetings, odd phrasing, mismatched logos, and a reply-to address that differs from the sender are all clues. But polished text is not proof of safety, since AI now writes fluent phishing.

3. How to recognize a fake website

A fake site copies the look of a real one to capture what you type. The page can be pixel-perfect, so judge the address, not the appearance. The part of a web address that matters is the registered domain, which sits just before the first single slash and is read right to left through the dots.

URL inspector

Type or paste any address, or pick an example. The tool highlights the real domain and lists warning signs. It works offline and never opens the link.

Padlock does not mean safe

HTTPS only means the connection is encrypted. Attackers get free certificates too, so a padlock says nothing about who owns the site.

Go there yourself

Instead of clicking, type the address or use a saved bookmark or the official app.

Trick addresses

Typosquatting (arnazon.com), extra words (paypal-security.com), lookalike characters (Cyrillic "а" vs Latin "a"), and brand names placed in subdomains.

Use a password manager

It fills credentials only on the exact saved domain. If it refuses to fill, treat that as an alarm.

5. Real-world examples

These are well documented incidents. Each began with a message aimed at a person.

2013

Target: an HVAC supplier as the way in

Attackers phished a refrigeration and HVAC contractor that worked with the US retailer Target, stole its network credentials, and eventually reached Target's payment systems. Around 40 million payment cards were exposed.

Lesson: your supply chain is part of your attack surface, and one stolen login can open a path to the crown jewels.
2013–15

Fake invoices at Google and Facebook

A Lithuanian man impersonated a real hardware supplier, sending forged invoices, contracts and emails to accounts payable staff at both companies. Together they paid out over $100 million before the fraud was caught, and most of it was later recovered.

Lesson: even technology giants lose money to convincing paperwork. Verify payment or bank detail changes by phone using a known number.
2016

The Podesta email compromise

The campaign chairman of a US presidential campaign received a fake "Google security alert" telling him to change his password. The link led to a lookalike page, and thousands of emails were later leaked. A shortened link hid the real destination.

Lesson: security alerts are a favorite disguise. Open the real site yourself rather than following the link.
2020

Twitter: a phone-based social engineering attack

Attackers phoned Twitter employees pretending to be internal IT and steered them to a fake login page. With the stolen credentials they took over high-profile accounts and ran a cryptocurrency scam.

Lesson: vishing works on technical staff too. Call back through an internal directory number, not the caller's.
2022

Uber: MFA fatigue

After obtaining a contractor's password, an attacker repeatedly triggered multi-factor approval prompts, then messaged the victim on WhatsApp pretending to be IT and telling them to approve one. They did, and the attacker got in.

Lesson: never approve a prompt you did not start. Report unexpected MFA requests immediately.

6. Best practices to avoid falling victim

Tick each habit you commit to adopting. Nothing is saved or sent anywhere.

    If you clicked or entered something

    Mistakes happen, and speed matters more than embarrassment. Reporting quickly is the most valuable thing you can do.

    1. Stop. Do not keep interacting with the page, file or caller.
    2. Disconnect the device from the network if you opened a suspicious attachment.
    3. Report it right away to your IT or security team, and use the report-phishing button if your mail client has one.
    4. Change the password for the affected account (and anywhere it was reused) from a clean device, and revoke active sessions.
    5. Watch for follow-up. Check bank and account activity, and tell the finance team if money or bank details were involved.
